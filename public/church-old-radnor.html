    St Stephen's, Old Radnor

    Home to the UK's oldest free-standing organ case, beneath the hills of Radnor Forest.

    Worship follows Common Worship Order One for Communion Services. We're privileged to possess an extremely fine organ, with the oldest free-standing organ case in the UK. Ruth, our talented organist (shared with Kinnerton), leads the singing. On the third Sunday, our evening worship — "Junior Praise" — is less formal and designed to be relevant to the young and young at heart.

    History & architecture

    The present building is mainly 15th-century, rebuilt after the church was burned during the Glyndŵr Rising of 1401–2, on the site of a possibly 6th-century earlier foundation. It was originally an important collegiate church — its "handsome proportions, balance, and size," as J. B. Sinclair and R. W. D. Fenn put it in Old Radnor Parish Church: A Short History, suggest it was "designed to be rather more than a parish church." It was restored in 1882 by the architect F. Preedy of Worcester. St Stephen's is listed Grade I by Cadw (building ID 9131, listed 1993).

    Although the church stands in Powys, it has remained part of the Church of England's Diocese of Hereford rather than the Church in Wales — parishioners voted 344–99 in a 1915/16 referendum to stay with the Church of England after Welsh disestablishment.

    Font

    A large, primitive circular stone font — possibly as old as the 8th century, and one of the few furnishings to survive the 1401–2 fire — has been called by one historic guide "probably the largest and oldest font in Britain."

    Rood screen

    The 15th-century rood screen — "one of the finest screens in Wales," Sinclair and Fenn call it — was carved by a school of Gloucestershire craftsmen whose work can also be seen at Cirencester parish church. It was originally painted and gilded, but both were stripped away when the screen was restored in 1858.

    Organ

    The organ case is thought to date from around 1500–1530, making it possibly the oldest free-standing organ case in the UK — Sinclair and Fenn go further, calling it "the earliest surviving organ case in the British Isles." Its authenticity was once questioned by the organ scholar Lady Susi Jeans, who suggested it might be a 19th-century assemblage, but a later inspection by the respected organ historian Stephen Bicknell concluded that the case is genuine, and survives close to its original state. The parish had a strong musical tradition to match: John Bull — composer, organist to the Chapel Royal, and the first Professor of Music at Gresham College, London — was born in Old Radnor in 1563.

    Monuments & other features

    Other survivals include 15th-century window glass depicting St Catherine and Edward IV's "white rose en soleil" badge, medieval floor tiles, a medieval vestments chest, an Easter Sepulchre, and an 18th-century monument to the Cornewall-Lewis family. A stone in the nave floor is said to be the tombstone of a 13th-century rector, Hugh Mortimer, who — Sinclair and Fenn record — was granted a licence in 1270 "to hunt with his own dogs, the fox, the hare, the badger, and the cat through the forests of Oxford, Gloucester, and Worcester." The tower's crenellated parapet has cross-shaped arrow-loops, which most scholarship regards as decorative rather than genuinely defensive. The ring of six bells, cast in 1724 by Abraham Rudhall of Gloucester, is still rung today; the tenor — the largest bell in the county — carries the inscription "I to the Church the living call and to the grave do summon all."
