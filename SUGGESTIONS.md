# Suggestions

Research-derived suggestions, not yet reviewed or actioned. Compiled by
researching each of the five churches against Wikipedia, Historic England /
Cadw listings, Wikidata, and other sources, then comparing against what
`church-*.html` and `our-churches.html` currently say. Nothing here has been
checked against the site's own editorial judgement yet — go through it and
move anything worth doing into `BACKLOG.md`.

Each item is tagged:
- **[correction]** — something the site currently states that looks wrong or overstated
- **[addition]** — a well-sourced fact currently missing from the page
- **[verify]** — plausible but only found in one source, or otherwise needs a second opinion before publishing
- **[legend]** — a real, citable tradition — but tradition, not documented fact — only worth adding if clearly framed as such

## St Mary's, Kinnerton

- **[correction/caution]** The page currently states no listing grade — **keep it that way**. Extensive searching found no evidence this church is actually Cadw-listed at all: it's absent from the full roster of 75 listed buildings/structures for the Old Radnor community (which does include neighbouring listed farmhouses and both other churches). Don't add a grade unless confirmed directly with Cadw — the page's level of architectural detail could easily read as implying statutory listing when it may not have any.
- **[verify]** The architect is credited as **Thomas Nicholson** (Hereford Diocesan Architect, 1823–1895) — well-corroborated by three independent sources. One CPAT survey record hedges "perhaps by Stephen Williams" instead (a different, real architect, the Radnorshire county surveyor) — Nicholson remains the better-attested answer, but the alternative exists in the record.
- **[verify]** "Burgoyne," credited for enlarging/refitting the 1833–4 predecessor church, could not be independently corroborated in any source reached (CPAT, Coflein, Wikishire). Not necessarily wrong — if there's an original citation for this, keep it — but flag as currently unverified from external sources.
- **[addition]** Likely literary source for the page's detailed Pevsner-style description ("rock-faced masonry," "wagon ceiling," "stiff-leaf capitals"): Haslam, R. (1979) *Buildings of Wales: Powys*. Useful to know if you want to verify or extend the description further.
- **[addition]** Despite being in Powys, this is also a **Church of England** parish (Diocese of Hereford, benefice "Old Radnor with Kinnerton") — historically a **chapel of ease to Old Radnor**, never its own parish. The village name is first recorded as **"Kynardton" in 1304**. (St Stephen's, Old Radnor's own page now covers the same England/Wales ecclesiastical-border point — worth deciding whether Kinnerton repeats it or just cross-references.)
- **[addition]** Churchyard extended c.1943; a notable old yew tree stands north-east of the church; the interior has an **aumbry and piscina** flanking the altar — none currently mentioned.

## St Thomas à Becket, Huntington

- **[correction]** "First recorded in the Domesday Book" overstates what Domesday actually says. Domesday (1086) records **Huntington as part of the Kington manor complex** — 3 hides, held by Earl Harold in 1066, "waste" by 1086 — and says **nothing about a church**. The building's earliest fabric dates from c.1210–c.1300 per Historic England, 120–200+ years after Domesday. Recommend rephrasing to something like "Huntington itself is recorded in the Domesday Book (1086); the present church dates from around 1300" rather than implying the church building is Domesday-recorded.
- **[addition]** **Grade II\*** confirmed, list entry **1349556** ("Church of St Thomas of Canterbury, Huntington" — Historic England's own listing uses this name, not "à Becket," for what it's worth), first listed 16 October 1967. HE dates the fabric to **c.1300**, with alterations in the early 17th century and restorations in 1892 and the mid-20th century.
- **[addition]** The 1892 restoration was overseen by architect **F.R. Kempson**; a porch was added; a new chancel screen was dedicated by the Bishop of Hereford that year.
- **[addition]** Two bells in the 17th-century tower: one by John Palmer of Gloucester (1671), one by Isaac Hadley of Leominster (1703) — currently unmentioned.
- **[addition]** A **sundial dated 1639** on the south-east corner; Elizabethan silver chalices (unhallmarked) and pewter vessels; Elizabethan pews with trefoil ends plus later box pews — more specific and interesting than the current generic "ancient oak pews."
- **[legend]** Local tradition holds the church was built around 1210 by **Richard le Bret**, one of the four knights who killed Thomas Becket in 1170, as an act of penance. A specific, citable legend — but genuinely just a legend, not documented fact, so should be framed explicitly as tradition if added (e.g. "reputed to have been built...").
- **[verify]** The Henry Payne stained-glass attribution and the "banker turned organ builder" framing of Thomas Casson both trace back to the church's own promotional copy rather than an independent source — Payne (1868–1940, Birmingham Group) is a real, well-documented artist, but no third-party source confirms which specific Huntington window is his; and Casson himself likely wasn't the banker (that was his father/uncle's trade), so that phrase could be lightly reworded.
- **[addition]** More organ detail available from the church's own site if wanted: original 5 stops at 8ft pitch with octave coupler, hand-pumped; the recent rebuild expanded it to 10 stops, giving "the effect of a two-manual organ with pedals."
