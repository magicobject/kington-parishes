# Suggestions

Research-derived suggestions, not yet reviewed or actioned. Compiled by
researching each of the five churches against Wikipedia, Historic England /
Cadw listings, Wikidata, and other sources, then comparing against what
`church-*.html` and `our-churches.html` currently say. Nothing here has been
checked against the site's own editorial judgement yet — go through it and
move anything worth doing into `BACKLOG.md`.

Each item is tagged:
- **[correction]** — something the site currently states that looks wrong or overstated
- **[addition]** — a well-sourced fact currently missing from the page
- **[verify]** — plausible but only found in one source, or otherwise needs a second opinion before publishing
- **[legend]** — a real, citable tradition — but tradition, not documented fact — only worth adding if clearly framed as such

## St Mary the Virgin, Kington

- **[correction]** The photo caption/alt-text says the church is "set among trees in a wooded valley." Multiple sources (Historic England-derived text, local history blogs) instead describe it standing **on a hill overlooking the town, near the site of the former Kington Castle** — worth checking the actual photo against this before keeping the caption as-is.
- **[correction]** The page calls it a "tall broach spire." Historic England's own listing description uses the more specific term **"double-broach spire (rebuilt 1794)"** — worth tightening if we want to match the statutory description.
- **[addition]** No listing grade is mentioned anywhere on the page. It's **Grade I**, list entry **1208031**, first listed 8 October 1953 — [Historic England](https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1208031).
- **[addition]** Construction history from the HE listing: c.1200 detached defensive tower → c.1300 nave/aisles → early C14 chancel rebuilt + south porch → c.1320 Decorated-style south chapel → 1794 spire rebuilt → c.1874 outer north aisle added.
- **[addition]** Norman circular font with rope moulding and incised zig-zag ornament (per HE listing) — currently unmentioned.
- **[addition]** Alabaster tomb-chest of **Sir Thomas Vaughan** (d. 1469, killed at the Battle of Edgecote fighting for the Yorkists) and his wife Ellen (Elena) Gethin, of nearby Hergest Court — effigies with 10×4 alabaster angels holding shields, restored 1845. A genuinely striking feature currently absent from the page.
- **[addition]** Memorial tablet to William Matthews (d. 1688), per the HE listing.
- **[addition]** The **lychgate** is separately **Grade II\* listed** (18th-century, carved stone, domed roof) — currently unmentioned anywhere.
- **[addition]** Ring of six bells — five by Rudhall of Gloucester (1736–39), one by William Evans of Chepstow (1764), refurbished 1978. Corroborated independently by the Hereford Diocesan Guild of Bellringers.
- **[verify]** Organ: said to be installed 1883 (J.W. Walker & Sons), rebuilt 1959 (Henry Willis & Sons) — only found on Wikipedia, not independently cross-checked.
- **[verify]** Local-history claim that Lady Margaret Hawkins (wife of Sir John Hawkins, of the Vaughan family) endowed the church and required Lady Hawkins School pupils to attend Sunday services and report on the sermon — a specific, good story, but only found in one source (Herefordshire Past). Worth checking against the Lady Hawkins School's own history before using.
- **[verify]** Naming: Historic England and Wikipedia both use plain **"Church of St Mary"** (no "the Virgin"); the Church of England's own "A Church Near You" directory uses "St Mary the Virgin" like we do. Not an error either way, just worth knowing the two heritage-authority sources drop "the Virgin."

## St Peter's, Titley

- **[addition]** No listing grade mentioned. It's **Grade II**, list entry **1081465**, listed 17 December 1986 (confirmed via Wikidata; Historic England and British Listed Buildings both blocked automated fetching, so pull the full listing description manually from https://historicengland.org.uk/listing/the-list/list-entry/1081465 if you want the verbatim text).
- **[addition]** The site has occupied this spot since **c.1120**, as a priory — a cell of the **Tironensian Order** (a Benedictine branch), one of only four such houses in England. There's a short separate Wikipedia stub on ["Titley Priory"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titley_Priory).
- **[addition]** The **west tower dates from c.1834**, designed and paid for by **Lady Elizabeth Coffin Greenly** of Titley Court; she and her mother are buried beneath it.
- **[verify]** The present church's main rebuild date is given as **1865** by the National Churches Trust but **1868** by Herefordshire Past — worth resolving before quoting a specific year. No architect has been identified for this rebuild in any source found (only the earlier 1834 tower has a named patron).
- **[addition]** Notable churchyard monument: **General Lázár Mészáros**, a Hungarian national hero (Supreme Commander of the Army and Minister of National Defence in Hungary's 1849 revolutionary government), died at Titley and was buried here in November 1858. A memorial plaque was unveiled in November 2019 by the Hungarian Embassy.
- **[verify]** Multiple sources say Mészáros's remains were **exhumed in 1991 and reburied at Baja Cathedral, Hungary** — if adding the grave to the site, phrase it as a grave/memorial rather than implying his remains are still there.

## St Stephen's, Old Radnor

- **[addition]** No listing grade mentioned. It's **Grade I** (Cadw, not Historic England — this one's in Wales), building ID **9131**, listed 1993.
- **[addition]** The current building is mainly **15th-century**, rebuilt after being burned during the **Glyndŵr Rising (1401/2)**, on the site of a possibly **6th-century** earlier foundation. It was originally a **collegiate church**. Restored in 1882 by architect F. Preedy of Worcester.
- **[addition]** A large, primitive **pre-Norman circular stone font** — possibly as old as the 8th century, and one that survived the 1401/2 fire — has been called by an old "Red Guide to Wales" **"probably the largest and oldest font in Britain."** This is currently completely unmentioned, despite being arguably as strong a headline claim as the organ case.
- **[addition]** A 15th-century **rood screen**, repeatedly described as one of the finest surviving in Wales — also unmentioned.
- **[addition]** Other unmentioned features: 15th-century window glass (St Catherine; Edward IV's "white rose en soleil" badge), medieval floor tiles, a medieval vestments chest, an Easter Sepulchre, an 18th-century Cornewall-Lewis monument, and a nave floor slab said to be the tombstone of 13th-century rector Hugh Mortimer. The tower's crenellations have cross-shaped arrow-loops — decorative rather than genuinely defensive; whether this counts as a "fortified church" is explicitly disputed in the literature.
- **[verify]** The "oldest free-standing organ case in the UK" claim is well-supported (organ historian Stephen Bicknell dates the case to c.1500–1530, pre-dating the Dissolution) but **was historically disputed** — organ scholar Lady Susi Jeans argued it might be a 19th-century assemblage of odd woodwork; Bicknell's own inspection concluded it's genuine. Worth citing Bicknell for authority rather than stating the claim as flatly uncontested.
- **[addition]** Worth a line somewhere on the site: although physically in Powys, this is a **Church of England** parish (Diocese of Hereford), not Church in Wales — parishioners voted 344–99 in a 1915/16 referendum to stay with the C of E after Welsh disestablishment. Same is true of Kinnerton (see below) — this might be worth one shared mention on `our-churches.html` rather than repeating per page.

## St Mary's, Kinnerton

- **[correction/caution]** The page currently states no listing grade — **keep it that way**. Extensive searching found no evidence this church is actually Cadw-listed at all: it's absent from the full roster of 75 listed buildings/structures for the Old Radnor community (which does include neighbouring listed farmhouses and both other churches). Don't add a grade unless confirmed directly with Cadw — the page's level of architectural detail could easily read as implying statutory listing when it may not have any.
- **[verify]** The architect is credited as **Thomas Nicholson** (Hereford Diocesan Architect, 1823–1895) — well-corroborated by three independent sources. One CPAT survey record hedges "perhaps by Stephen Williams" instead (a different, real architect, the Radnorshire county surveyor) — Nicholson remains the better-attested answer, but the alternative exists in the record.
- **[verify]** "Burgoyne," credited for enlarging/refitting the 1833–4 predecessor church, could not be independently corroborated in any source reached (CPAT, Coflein, Wikishire). Not necessarily wrong — if there's an original citation for this, keep it — but flag as currently unverified from external sources.
- **[addition]** Likely literary source for the page's detailed Pevsner-style description ("rock-faced masonry," "wagon ceiling," "stiff-leaf capitals"): Haslam, R. (1979) *Buildings of Wales: Powys*. Useful to know if you want to verify or extend the description further.
- **[addition]** Despite being in Powys, this is also a **Church of England** parish (Diocese of Hereford, benefice "Old Radnor with Kinnerton") — historically a **chapel of ease to Old Radnor**, never its own parish. The village name is first recorded as **"Kynardton" in 1304**.
- **[addition]** Churchyard extended c.1943; a notable old yew tree stands north-east of the church; the interior has an **aumbry and piscina** flanking the altar — none currently mentioned.

## St Thomas à Becket, Huntington

- **[correction]** "First recorded in the Domesday Book" overstates what Domesday actually says. Domesday (1086) records **Huntington as part of the Kington manor complex** — 3 hides, held by Earl Harold in 1066, "waste" by 1086 — and says **nothing about a church**. The building's earliest fabric dates from c.1210–c.1300 per Historic England, 120–200+ years after Domesday. Recommend rephrasing to something like "Huntington itself is recorded in the Domesday Book (1086); the present church dates from around 1300" rather than implying the church building is Domesday-recorded.
- **[addition]** **Grade II\*** confirmed, list entry **1349556** ("Church of St Thomas of Canterbury, Huntington" — Historic England's own listing uses this name, not "à Becket," for what it's worth), first listed 16 October 1967. HE dates the fabric to **c.1300**, with alterations in the early 17th century and restorations in 1892 and the mid-20th century.
- **[addition]** The 1892 restoration was overseen by architect **F.R. Kempson**; a porch was added; a new chancel screen was dedicated by the Bishop of Hereford that year.
- **[addition]** Two bells in the 17th-century tower: one by John Palmer of Gloucester (1671), one by Isaac Hadley of Leominster (1703) — currently unmentioned.
- **[addition]** A **sundial dated 1639** on the south-east corner; Elizabethan silver chalices (unhallmarked) and pewter vessels; Elizabethan pews with trefoil ends plus later box pews — more specific and interesting than the current generic "ancient oak pews."
- **[legend]** Local tradition holds the church was built around 1210 by **Richard le Bret**, one of the four knights who killed Thomas Becket in 1170, as an act of penance. A specific, citable legend — but genuinely just a legend, not documented fact, so should be framed explicitly as tradition if added (e.g. "reputed to have been built...").
- **[verify]** The Henry Payne stained-glass attribution and the "banker turned organ builder" framing of Thomas Casson both trace back to the church's own promotional copy rather than an independent source — Payne (1868–1940, Birmingham Group) is a real, well-documented artist, but no third-party source confirms which specific Huntington window is his; and Casson himself likely wasn't the banker (that was his father/uncle's trade), so that phrase could be lightly reworded.
- **[addition]** More organ detail available from the church's own site if wanted: original 5 stops at 8ft pitch with octave coupler, hand-pumped; the recent rebuild expanded it to 10 stops, giving "the effect of a two-manual organ with pedals."
