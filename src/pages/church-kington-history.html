St Mary the Virgin, Kington

History & Architecture

Eight centuries of building, from the Norman tower to a stained-glass window installed in 1995 — a fuller history of St Mary's than fits on the church's own page.

A church on an ancient site

St Mary's is one of more than 2,500 churches in England dedicated to Our Lady. It stands on rising ground at the western edge of the town, close to the site of Kington Castle, founded in the reign of Henry I (1100–1135). The church dates from the late Norman period, and is listed Grade I (Historic England list entry 1208031, first listed 8 October 1953).

The tower & spire

The oldest part of the building is the tower, erected around 1200. Like many church towers on the Welsh borders — the Marches — it was built to double as a defensive stronghold, evidenced by walls six feet thick. It was originally detached from the rest of the church, and is now linked to the south aisle by a round-headed Norman arch.

The external stair to the belfry is a Victorian addition. At the time it was built, the bell-ringers were moved out of the tower's ground floor so that a vestry could be created there, where the parson could prepare himself for services.

The original spire was destroyed by lightning in 1793 and rebuilt the following year in its present form: a pair of truncated pyramidal steps topped by a broach spire.

Bells

The belfry, in the south tower, holds a ring of six bells, all dating from the eighteenth century: five cast by Rudhall of Gloucester between 1736 and 1739, and one by William Evans of Chepstow in 1764. The ring was refurbished in 1978.

The Nave

The nave — early 13th century — is the main body of the church. At least 50 different stonemasons worked on its construction, as seen in the various masons' marks left on the stone pillars and arches throughout the nave, the chancel arch, and the south chapel and south aisle (one, near the eagle lectern, is a simple crossed groove). The word "nave" comes from the Latin navis, meaning ship — it was thought the nave roof resembled an upside-down ship, or the Ark of Salvation.

Maintaining the nave was originally the responsibility of the congregation, not the clergy, and it served many purposes beyond worship: the scene of trial by ordeal, storage space for the weapons every parish was required to provide (the regimental colours found in many churches are a reminder of this), a venue for guild plays, processions, church ales and business, and shelter for pilgrims and travellers. Permanent benches or pews only became common in the late 16th century, as preaching — and the need for a seated congregation — increased.

Font

The 12th-century Norman font, in tub style, has rope moulding at its base and zigzag carving round the rim. Set into the floor nearby is a baptismal tank, added in the second half of the 17th century so that people of "riper years" could be baptised — it saw regular use for adult baptism in the eighteenth century.

The Chancel

The chancel was added later in the 13th century, in the Early English style, with a trio of large lancets making up the east window and six smaller lancet windows in the north wall. The south wall has a large 13th-century window with attractive "Y" tracery. The chancel arch is also Early English, from the late 13th century. The chancel windows were filled with blue glass in 1862 — it has been suggested that the predominant blueness across the church's stained glass is a gesture to St Mary, its patron saint.

High altar

The present high altar is a free-standing communion table, which replaced the pre-Reformation stone altar and allows the priest to face the congregation when celebrating Holy Communion.

Piscina

A piscina survives in the chancel wall — the basin where the sacred vessels were once washed after Communion. "Piscina" is a Latin word which originally meant "fish-pond".

South chapel & the Vaughan effigies

The chapel on the south side of the chancel is 14th-century Decorated Gothic in style. It holds the church's most striking historic feature: the 15th-century alabaster effigies of Sir Thomas Vaughan of nearby Hergest Court and his wife. Sir Thomas, a supporter of the Yorkist cause, was killed at the Battle of Edgecote Moor, near Banbury, in 1469; his body was brought back to Kington, where he now lies alongside his wife, Ellen Gethin — known as "Ellen the Terrible". The effigies, though they have undergone restoration (the tomb was restored in 1845), remain wonderfully carved and detailed, and the base of the tomb chest is ringed with ten carved alabaster angels holding heraldic shields.

A memorial tablet to William Matthews (d. 1688) also survives in the church. Lady Margaret Hawkins — of the Vaughan family, and wife of the Elizabethan naval commander Sir John Hawkins — is thought to have endowed the church, with her bequest said to have required pupils of the adjoining Lady Hawkins School to attend Sunday services.

Organ

The organ was made originally by Mr Nicholson of Worcester in 1848, at a cost of £310. In 1884 it was enlarged and rebuilt by Walkers of London, and placed in the Vaughan (South) Chapel. It was moved to its present position in the church in 1909 and, in 2024, was completely rebuilt.

North aisle

The north aisle is a 19th-century addition; the outer north aisle, now home to the kitchen and chair vestry, followed around 1874.

Reredos & screen

The oak reredos is a memorial to prebendary Charles Craigie (1888–1913). The screen was moved from its original position in the chancel to its present position, in this chapel in the north nave.

Stained glass

Most of the church's stained glass dates from the second half of the 19th century and the second half of the 20th. The east window's large right-hand lancet was filled by the famed Victorian firm Clayton and Bell, and the chancel windows were glazed in blue in 1862. Elsewhere, windows depict the twelve apostles. The most recently installed window (1995) is the work of local stained-glass specialist Jim Budd, and depicts the Holy Spirit as a dove. A separate guide to the church's stained glass windows is available in the church itself.

Pulpit

The pulpit is mid-Victorian, with a decorative metalwork handrail.

The churchyard & lychgate

In medieval times, a feature of the churchyard was its preaching cross; it is assumed the cross acquired its now-dilapidated condition during the Reformation. It now serves as a focus for the church's Garden of Remembrance.

The elegant lychgate belongs to the late 18th century, and is separately listed Grade II*. The Lych Gate is where, traditionally, a coffin was set down to await the welcome and blessing of the parish priest before a funeral.
