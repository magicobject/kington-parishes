St Stephen's, Old Radnor

Home to the UK's oldest free-standing organ case, beneath the hills of Radnor Forest.

Worship follows Common Worship Order One for Communion Services. We're privileged to possess an extremely fine organ, with the oldest free-standing organ case in the UK. Ruth, our talented organist (shared with Kinnerton), leads the singing. On the third Sunday, our evening worship — "Junior Praise" — is less formal and designed to be relevant to the young and young at heart.

History & architecture

The present building is mainly 15th-century, rebuilt after the church was burned during the Glyndŵr Rising of 1401–2, on the site of a possibly 6th-century earlier foundation. It was originally an important collegiate church, and was restored in 1882 by the architect F. Preedy of Worcester. St Stephen's is listed Grade I by Cadw (building ID 9131, listed 1993).

Although the church stands in Powys, it has remained part of the Church of England's Diocese of Hereford rather than the Church in Wales — parishioners voted 344–99 in a 1915/16 referendum to stay with the Church of England after Welsh disestablishment.

Font

A large, primitive circular stone font — possibly as old as the 8th century, and one of the few furnishings to survive the 1401–2 fire — has been called by one historic guide "probably the largest and oldest font in Britain."

Rood screen

The 15th-century rood screen is widely regarded as one of the finest surviving in Wales.

Organ

The organ case is thought to date from around 1500–1530, making it possibly the oldest free-standing organ case in the UK. Its authenticity was once questioned by the organ scholar Lady Susi Jeans, who suggested it might be a 19th-century assemblage, but a later inspection by the respected organ historian Stephen Bicknell concluded that the case is genuine, and survives close to its original state.

Monuments & other features

Other survivals include 15th-century window glass depicting St Catherine and Edward IV's "white rose en soleil" badge, medieval floor tiles, a medieval vestments chest, an Easter Sepulchre, and an 18th-century monument to the Cornewall-Lewis family. A stone in the nave floor is said to be the tombstone of a 13th-century rector, Hugh Mortimer. The tower's crenellated parapet has cross-shaped arrow-loops, which most scholarship regards as decorative rather than genuinely defensive.
